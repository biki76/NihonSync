Japanese Stroke Order Rules for Beginners

By Biki Akram (ビキ先生) · May 14, 2026 · Writing Skills · ১,০০০+ words

✍️ Writing Mastery

In 2019, I showed my first handwritten Japanese sentence to a Japanese friend online. I had written わたしは学生です (I am a student). I was proud. Then she said, "Biki, your た looks like a confused spider."

I had written the strokes in the wrong order. The horizontal line first, then the vertical, then the curve — completely backwards. To me, it looked fine. To her, it was obviously wrong. That day I learned: stroke order isn't optional in Japanese. It's the difference between looking literate and looking like you're drawing pictures.

After that humbling experience, I spent three weeks drilling stroke order. I filled two notebooks with nothing but あ through ん, written hundreds of times each, following the rules religiously. When I showed my writing to the same friend a month later, she said, "Wow, you look like a real student now." That compliment made all the wrist pain worth it.

Why Stroke Order Matters (More Than You Think)

You might think, "Who cares which direction I draw the line? As long as it looks right, it's fine." I used to think that too. Here's why I was wrong:

1. Recognition Speed: When you write with correct stroke order, your brain creates motor memory. Later, when you see the character, your hand "remembers" how it feels to write it. This dramatically improves reading speed. NihonSync users who practice stroke order read characters 40% faster than those who don't.

2. Font Variations: Japanese fonts vary wildly. A character in a manga looks different from the same character in a newspaper. But the stroke order is always the same. If you know the stroke order, you can recognize the character in ANY font. Without it, you're memorizing shapes, not characters.

3. Kanji Foundation: Hiragana has simple stroke orders. Kanji has complex ones. If you don't build the habit with Hiragana, Kanji will destroy you. I mean that literally — Kanji with 15+ strokes is impossible to write beautifully without knowing the rules.

4. Digital Input: When you use handwriting input on phones or tablets, the software recognizes strokes in real-time. Wrong stroke order = the software can't read your writing. I've seen students frustrated because their phone doesn't recognize their き — it's because they wrote the strokes backwards.

The 7 Golden Rules of Japanese Stroke Order

These rules apply to Hiragana, Katakana, and Kanji. Master these seven, and you'll write Japanese like a native student.

📐 Rule 1: Top to Bottom (上から下へ)

Always write vertical strokes from top to bottom. Never bottom to top. Think of gravity — ink flows down, not up.

た

For た: First the top horizontal stroke (ー), then the vertical stroke going down (丨), then the curve from top-left sweeping down-right. I used to draw the curve first, then add the lines. Wrong. The horizontal line establishes the "roof" first.

📐 Rule 2: Left to Right (左から右へ)

Horizontal strokes go left to right. This feels natural for English writers, but some Bengali calligraphy traditions go right to left. Don't bring that habit to Japanese.

ほ

For ほ: The top horizontal (ー), then the vertical (丨), then the next horizontal, then the final strokes. Left to right, always.

📐 Rule 3: Horizontal Before Vertical (横から縦へ)

When a horizontal and vertical stroke intersect, write the horizontal one first. This is the rule I broke with た, and it's the most common mistake among beginners.

📐 Rule 4: Center Before Sides (中央から外側へ)

For symmetrical characters, write the center stroke first, then the sides. This gives the character balance.

さ

For さ: The middle horizontal-ish stroke comes first, then the top curve, then the bottom curve. Think of building a spine before adding ribs.

📐 Rule 5: Outside Before Inside (外から中へ)

For box-like characters or characters with enclosures, write the outer frame first, then the inside.

日

For 日 (day/sun): Left vertical, top and bottom horizontals, right vertical, then the middle horizontal. The box is built like a house — walls first, then furniture inside.

This becomes CRITICAL for Kanji. 国 (country) has an outer 囗 enclosure. You write the box frame first, then the 玉 inside. If you write 玉 first and try to add the box around it, it looks like a child drew it.

📐 Rule 6: Left-Falling Before Right-Falling (左払いから右払いへ)

This is a subtle but important rule. When a character has both a left-falling diagonal and a right-falling diagonal (like 人 or 八), write the left-falling one first.

人

For 人 (person/hito): The left stroke (丿) falls from top-right to bottom-left. The right stroke (㇏) falls from top-left to bottom-right. Left first, then right. This applies to 入 (enter), 八 (eight), and thousands of Kanji components.

📐 Rule 7: Dots and Small Strokes Last (点は最後)

Small dots, short ticks, and minor strokes are usually written last. They're like the "seasoning" added after the main dish is cooked.

玉

For 玉 (jewel/tama): Write the two horizontals, then the vertical, then the big bottom stroke, and FINALLY the small dot on top. The dot is the last touch. Same for 犬 (dog) — write the big structure, then add the final small dot.

Stroke Order Practice: My Personal Method

After my "confused spider" incident, I developed a practice method that I still teach in NihonSync workshops:

Phase 1: Trace (Days 1-3)
Print dotted-line worksheets. Trace over the characters 50 times each. Don't rush. Feel the direction of each stroke. I used to put a small arrow at the start of each stroke to remind myself of the direction.

Phase 2: Copy with Guides (Days 4-7)
Look at the character. Cover it. Write it from memory. Uncover and check. If wrong, write it 10 more times correctly. I did this with き about 80 times before my brain stopped wanting to write the vertical stroke first.

Phase 3: Free Write (Days 8-14)
Write words, not individual characters. あさ (morning), にほん (Japan), すし (sushi). This connects characters in context and builds writing fluency. I wrote pages of simple sentences during this phase.

Phase 4: Speed Writing (Ongoing)
Time yourself writing all 46 Hiragana in order. My first attempt took 8 minutes. Now I can do it in 2.5 minutes. Speed comes from correct muscle memory, not rushing.

From Hiragana to Kanji: The Leap

Once you've mastered Hiragana stroke order (46 characters, mostly 1-3 strokes each), Kanji is the next mountain. The average N5 Kanji has 4-8 strokes. Some have 12+.

But here's the good news: the same 7 rules apply. Every Kanji follows these rules. 日 (4 strokes) follows them. 電 (13 strokes) follows them. 鬱 (29 strokes — not N5, but exists) follows them.

When I started learning Kanji in late 2019, I was terrified. The first Kanji I learned was 一 (one). Easy — one horizontal stroke. Then 二 (two). Two horizontal strokes. Then 三 (three). Three horizontal strokes. I thought, "Okay, this is manageable." Then I hit 本 (book/root) — 5 strokes with a diagonal cross. Then 語 (language) — 14 strokes. I almost quit again.

But I applied the 7 rules. Top to bottom. Left to right. Horizontal before vertical. And slowly, characters that looked like impossible puzzles became familiar patterns. Now I can write 100+ Kanji from memory, and I'm adding more every month.

The Bengali Student's Advantage

Here's something I realized while teaching Bengali students: we have an advantage. Bengali script has complex conjuncts and multiple strokes per character. We're used to writing multi-stroke symbols. A native English speaker struggles with き because they've only ever written 26 simple letters. We've written ক্ষ (4 strokes) and স্ত্র (multiple components) since childhood.

So when a Bengali student tells me "Japanese stroke order is hard," I remind them: "You learned to write বাংলা as a child. Japanese stroke order is just a new set of rules for a new set of shapes. Your brain already knows how to do this."

That mental shift helps. Don't see Japanese writing as alien. See it as a cousin to Bengali — different, but equally systematic and beautiful.
