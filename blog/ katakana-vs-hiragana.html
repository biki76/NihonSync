Katakana vs Hiragana: When to Use Which?

By Biki Akram (ビキ先生) · May 14, 2026 · Writing Systems · ১,০৫০+ words

📝 Writing Systems

I'll never forget the day I walked into a Japanese convenience store in Tokyo (well, a video of one — I haven't actually been to Japan yet, but let me set the scene). I looked at the shelves and saw three different writing systems fighting for space. おにぎり in Hiragana. コーヒー in Katakana. 日本 in Kanji. My brain short-circuited. Why? Why three systems? And more importantly, why is coffee written in Katakana but rice ball in Hiragana?

This question haunted me for months. I would message my Japanese language exchange partner with screenshots: "Why is this in Katakana?" "Why is that in Hiragana?" She was patient, but I could feel her thinking, "This guy asks a lot of questions." Eventually, the patterns clicked. And now, after teaching thousands of Bengali students, I can explain the Hiragana-Katakana divide in a way that makes immediate sense.

The Simple Rule (That Works 90% of the Time)

🌸 Hiragana (ひらがな)

Soft, curved, flowing characters. Used for:

• Native Japanese words
• Grammar particles (は, が, を, に)
• Verb and adjective endings
• Words without Kanji
• Children's books

⚡ Katakana (カタカナ)

Sharp, angular, straight characters. Used for:

• Foreign loan words
• Scientific names
• Animal and plant names
• Onomatopoeia (sound words)
• Emphasis (like italics)

Think of it this way: Hiragana is Japanese wearing traditional clothes. Katakana is Japanese wearing a suit. Both are Japanese, but one feels native and warm, the other feels imported and sharp.

The Five Katakana Rules (With Bengali Context)

Rule 1: Foreign Loan Words

This is the big one. Any word borrowed from another language gets Katakana. In Bangladesh, we do something similar — we say "টেবিল" (table), "চেয়ার" (chair), "বাস" (bus). These are English words adapted to Bengali pronunciation. Japanese does the same, but writes them in Katakana instead of modifying the spelling.

English "internet" → Japanese インターネット (intānetto)
English "computer" → Japanese コンピューター (konpyūtā)
English "hotel" → Japanese ホテル (hoteru)
বাংলায় আমরা "হোটেল" বলি, জাপানিরা ホテল বলে কিন্তু কাটাকানায় লেখে।

Rule 2: Animal and Plant Names

This one surprised me. In Japanese, animal and plant names are often written in Katakana, even if they're native species. It's not a hard rule, but it's common in scientific and educational contexts.

Dog: イヌ (inu) in Katakana for scientific use, いぬ in Hiragana for daily use
Cat: ネコ (neko) in Katakana, ねこ in Hiragana
দৈনন্দিন কথোপকথনে হিরাগানা, বিজ্ঞানের বইয়ে কাটাকানা।

Rule 3: Onomatopoeia (Sound Words)

Japanese LOVES sound words. And most of them are in Katakana. These are words that mimic sounds, feelings, or states.

Rule 4: Emphasis and Styling

In English, we use italics or bold for emphasis. In Japanese, they use Katakana. You'll see this in manga, advertisements, and casual writing.

Normal: すごい (amazing)
Emphasized: スゴイ (AMAZING!)
মাঙ্গায় প্রায়শই দেখবেন — সাধারণ শব্দ কাটাকানায় লিখে জোর দেওয়া হয়।

Rule 5: Names of Foreign People and Places

If you're from Bangladesh and introduce yourself in Japan, your name will be written in Katakana. My name Biki becomes ビキ. Bangladesh becomes バングラデシュ.

バングラデシュ (Banguradeshu) = Bangladesh
ダッカ (Dakka) = Dhaka
ビキ・アクラム (Biki Akuramu) = Biki Akram
বিদেশি নাম ও দেশের নাম সবসময় কাটাকানায় লেখা হয়।

When the Rules Break (And They Do)

Japanese is a living language, and living languages have exceptions. Here are situations where the rules get fuzzy:

1. Native Words in Katakana for Style: Some restaurants write サカナ (fish) in Katakana on their menu to look modern. It's not "correct" by textbook rules, but it's common.

2. Foreign Words in Hiragana for Cuteness: In cute character design or children's products, you might see foreign words written in Hiragana to look softer. てれび instead of テレビ for "TV" in a kindergarten context.

3. Historical Shift: Some words that were once foreign are now considered native. タバコ (tobacco/cigarette) is still Katakana, but ビール (beer) might appear in Hiragana in some casual contexts. The boundaries are shifting.

4. Personal Preference: Writers sometimes choose Hiragana or Katakana based on personal style. A novelist might write a foreign word in Hiragana to create a specific mood.

Biki's Practical Advice: For JLPT N5, don't worry about exceptions. The exam follows standard rules. Focus on the 90% that is predictable. Master the rules first, then enjoy the exceptions as you advance.

My Katakana Learning Story

I learned Hiragana first, like everyone should. Then I looked at Katakana and thought, "This looks like Hiragana's angry cousin." The shapes are similar but sharper. あ (Hiragana) vs ア (Katakana). か vs カ. さ vs サ. My brain kept mixing them up.

The breakthrough came when I stopped comparing them visually and started associating Katakana with foreign concepts. Every time I saw カ, I thought "coffee shop" (カフェ). Every time I saw ラ, I thought "radio" (ラジオ). This contextual association worked better than pure memorization.

Another trick: I wrote my English notes in Katakana. "I need to buy PAN and KOHI today." It looked ridiculous, but it forced my brain to switch to Katakana mode. After two weeks, I could read Katakana almost as fast as Hiragana.

Final Thoughts

Hiragana and Katakana aren't enemies — they're partners. Hiragana carries the soul of Japanese. Katakana brings the world into Japanese. Together with Kanji, they create one of the most expressive writing systems on Earth.

When I see a Japanese sentence now, I don't see chaos. I see a beautiful system: Kanji for meaning, Hiragana for grammar and native words, Katakana for foreign flavor. It's like a well-orchestrated symphony, and each script plays its part.

Learn both. Respect both. And one day, you'll walk into that convenience store (real or virtual) and read every label with confidence.
