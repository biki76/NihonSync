JLPT N5 Kanji: The First 50 You Need to Master

By Biki Akram (ビキ先生) · May 14, 2026 · Kanji Mastery · ১,২০০+ words · 50 Essential Kanji

🈶 Kanji Essential

I'll never forget the day I encountered my first Kanji in the wild. I was reading a simple Japanese children's book that I had borrowed from a friend. The sentence was supposed to be easy: 「今日はいい天気です。」(Today is good weather.) I knew 今日 was "today" because I had learned it. But then I saw 天気 and my brain shut down. Two complex characters that looked like tiny architectural drawings. What were they? How do you even start reading something that complicated?

I looked up 天気. It meant "weather." The first character 天 meant "heaven/sky." The second 気 meant "spirit/air." Together: "sky spirit" = weather. I thought, "That's beautiful but completely impractical." How was I supposed to remember that 天 (heaven) + 気 (spirit) = weather? And that was just ONE word. JLPT N5 requires about 100 Kanji. N1 requires 2,000+. The mountain seemed impossible.

But here's what I learned over five years: Kanji isn't memorization — it's pattern recognition. Once you see the components, the characters stop being random lines and start being logical puzzles. This guide covers the first 50 Kanji every N5 student must master, with the exact method I used to learn them.

Why Start With 50? (The Pareto Principle)

The Pareto principle says 20% of effort produces 80% of results. For N5 Kanji, the first 50 characters appear in approximately 75% of all N5 reading passages. Master these 50, and you'll recognize most Kanji in the exam. The remaining 50 are important but less frequent.

I divided the 50 into 5 groups of 10. Learn one group per week, and in 5 weeks, you'll have a solid Kanji foundation. This is the exact schedule I used and now teach in NihonSync.

My Kanji Learning Method: The Story Technique

Raw memorization failed me. What worked was creating stories for each Kanji. Let me share a few:

明 (あか / bright): This Kanji combines 日 (sun) and 月 (moon). Sun + Moon = bright! The sun and moon together light up the world. I imagine a bright Bangladeshi afternoon with the moon faintly visible.

休 (やす / rest): This combines 人 (person) and 木 (tree). A person leaning against a tree = resting! I picture myself resting under a mango tree in my grandmother's village.

林 (はやし / grove): Two 木 (tree) characters side by side. Two trees = a small forest/grove. Three trees 森 (もり) = a big forest. The more trees, the bigger the woods!

男 (おとこ / man): This combines 田 (rice field) and 力 (power). A man with power working in the rice field = the traditional image of masculinity in agricultural Japan. I think of my grandfather working in the paddy fields.

These stories might seem silly, but they create emotional connections. And emotional connections create memory. NihonSync's Kanji module uses visual mnemonics like these for every N5 character.

ON vs KUN Readings: The Double Life of Kanji

This is what confused me most as a beginner. Every Kanji has at least two readings:

ON Reading (音読み / on'yomi): The Chinese-derived reading. Used in compound words.
KUN Reading (訓読み / kun'yomi): The native Japanese reading. Used when the Kanji stands alone or with Hiragana endings.

Example with 山 (mountain):
ON: サン (san) → 富士山 (ふじさん / Mt. Fuji) · 山脈 (さんみゃく / mountain range)
KUN: やま (yama) → 山 (やま / mountain) · 山登り (やまのぼり / mountain climbing)

Same character, different readings depending on context. This is why Kanji is challenging — but also why it's beautiful. One symbol carries multiple layers of history.

Final Words

Kanji intimidated me for years. The first time I saw 鬱 (depression, 29 strokes), I thought, "I'll never learn this language." But I started with 一. Then 二. Then 三. Small steps. Consistent practice. And now I can read 100+ Kanji comfortably, with more added every month.

The 50 Kanji in this guide are your starting line, not your finish line. Master them, and you've built the foundation for 100, then 500, then 2,000. Every Kanji master started with 一.

One of my favorite quotes from a Japanese proverb: 塵も積もれば山となる (Chiri mo tsumoreba yama to naru) — "Even dust, when piled up, becomes a mountain." Every Kanji you learn is a speck of dust. Keep piling them up.
